\section{Analytical methods and mathematical scope}
\label{sec:methods}

The comparison uses one recorded customer cohort, feature contract, set of candidate grids, deployment constraints and evaluation measures. K Means provides a centre-based reference partition that can be approximated by ExKMC rules. CLASSIX provides a separate clustering result with native aggregation provenance. ExKMC is not a clustering algorithm, and no composite score ranks it against CLASSIX. The same change in represented information is traced through each partition and its corresponding explanation. Before any hyperparameter grid is given, this section first explains what each clustering method mathematically computes, because a grid of settings is only meaningful once the underlying optimisation and its dependence on representation are understood.

\subsection{What K Means computes}
\label{sec:kmeans-math}

K Means partitions the standardised customer vectors $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb{R}^d$ ($n=11{,}719$; $d=3$ for the compact representation or $d=12$ for the rich representation) into $k$ disjoint clusters. Writing $C_1,\dots,C_k$ for the clusters and $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k\in\mathbb{R}^d$ for their centroids, K Means seeks the assignment and centroids that minimise the within-cluster sum of squared Euclidean distances,
\[
\min_{C_1,\dots,C_k,\ \mu_1,\dots,\mu_k}\ \sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i\in C_j}\lVert x_i-\mu_j\rVert_2^2 .
\]
This is the objective originally proposed by \citet{macqueen1967kmeans}.
Given a fixed assignment, the centroid $\mu_j$ that minimises this objective for cluster $j$ is simply the coordinate-wise mean of the points assigned to it. Given fixed centroids, the assignment that minimises the objective places each point in the cluster whose centroid is nearest in Euclidean distance. This second property means K Means always induces a \emph{Voronoi partition} of the feature space: the region belonging to centroid $\mu_j$ is exactly the set of points closer to $\mu_j$ than to any other centroid, and these regions are convex polyhedral cells whose boundaries are the perpendicular bisectors between pairs of centroids. K Means is therefore a \emph{centroid-based} method operating entirely on Euclidean geometry: two customers are judged similar only insofar as their standardised feature vectors are numerically close under the ordinary Euclidean distance.

Minimising this objective exactly is NP-hard in general, so K Means alternates between the two steps above (reassign points to their nearest current centroid, then recompute centroids as cluster means) until neither step changes the partition. This procedure only ever reaches a \emph{local} optimum, and which local optimum it reaches depends on where the centroids started. \emph{k-means++} is a seeding rule that chooses the initial centroids sequentially, picking each new centroid at random with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centroid already chosen, so that the initial centroids are spread across the data rather than clustered together \citep{arthur2007kmeanspp}; this improves the expected quality of the local optimum reached and speeds convergence, but it does not guarantee that the global optimum is found. \emph{Multiple initialisations} — refitting from several independent k-means++ seeds and keeping the run with the lowest objective value — further reduce, without eliminating, the risk of settling on a poor local optimum. This dissertation uses k-means++ seeding, twenty initialisations and the fixed seed 20260801 for every K Means fit \citep{macqueen1967kmeans,arthur2007kmeanspp}, so that the residual variation across repeated fits with the same data and parameters is attributable only to the finite number of initialisations, not to an unfixed source of randomness.

Because the objective sums \emph{squared} per-dimension differences, K Means is directly sensitive to feature scaling: a feature recorded on a larger numeric scale, or with larger variance, can dominate the Euclidean distance purely because of its units, independently of how informative it actually is. This is why every feature, in both representations, is standardised to zero mean and unit variance before clustering (\S\ref{sec:feature-engineering}) — standardisation gives each of the three or twelve features comparable influence on the distance calculation, rather than letting the raw units of, say, a day count and a ratio determine their relative importance by accident. This dependence on scaling is also the direct mechanical link between representation and outcome that motivates this dissertation's central comparison: adding nine standardised behavioural dimensions to the three purchase dimensions changes \emph{every} pairwise Euclidean distance between customers, because Euclidean distance is computed jointly over all $d$ coordinates. A different set of pairwise distances can move where the objective-minimising centroids fall, and can therefore change which Voronoi cell — and so which final cluster — a given customer falls into, even though that customer's three original purchase coordinates are numerically identical in both the compact and the rich fit. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} traces this mechanical link through to the observed low cross-representation agreement (RQ1).

\subsection{What CLASSIX computes}
\label{sec:classix-math}

CLASSIX (\emph{fast and explainable clustering via sorting}) reaches a partition by a different route from K Means, and is introduced here in the stages it actually executes, before any of its hyperparameters are discussed \citep{chen2024classix}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Standardised observations.} As with K Means, CLASSIX operates on the same standardised customer vectors $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ used throughout this dissertation.
\item \textbf{Principal sorting direction.} CLASSIX computes a single unit vector $u\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the \emph{principal direction}: the direction along which the standardised data have maximum variance, equivalently the leading eigenvector of the data's covariance matrix (the same direction principal component analysis would select as its first component; \S\ref{sec:stability-certificate} below uses this same covariance matrix and its eigenvalues directly).
\item \textbf{Scalar projection.} Every observation is projected onto that one direction, giving a single number $s_i=u^{\mathsf T}x_i$, its \emph{projected score}. This collapses a $d$-dimensional point onto a one-dimensional line while preserving, approximately, its position along the direction of greatest spread in the data.
\item \textbf{Sorting observations.} All $n$ customers are sorted into ascending order of their projected score $s_i$. Customers with similar scores — and therefore, typically, similar positions along the principal direction — become neighbours in this sorted order.
\item \textbf{Aggregation around starting points.} CLASSIX walks through the sorted order. Whenever the current point does not lie within a fixed \emph{aggregation radius} $r$ (measured by ordinary Euclidean distance, in the full $d$-dimensional space) of the current group's \emph{starting point} — the first, representative point of that group — a new group is started, with the current point becoming the new starting point. \S\ref{sec:projection-filtering} below shows why the one-dimensional sorted score can be used to rule out many of these full-distance checks in advance.
\item \textbf{Aggregation groups.} The result of this walk is a collection of \emph{aggregation groups}: each group is a starting point together with every subsequent point (in sorted order) found to lie within radius $r$ of it. A dataset of $n$ points can produce anywhere from one aggregation group (if all points are mutually close) up to many small groups (if points are spread out); this dissertation's fitted customer data produce 131 groups in the compact representation and 559 in the rich representation (\S\ref{sec:explanation-results}).
\item \textbf{Merging groups.} Because aggregation only ever compares a point with its own group's starting point, two aggregation groups whose starting points are themselves close together are then merged into one cluster. CLASSIX offers two merge rules: \emph{distance merging}, which merges two groups only when the Euclidean distance between their starting points is below a merge threshold (the aggregation radius scaled by a configured merge factor), and \emph{density merging}, which instead merges two groups when the local density of observations found between their two starting points exceeds a threshold — a criterion that can join two groups whose starting points are further apart than the distance-merging threshold would allow, provided enough intervening points bridge them. \S\ref{sec:stability-certificate} below derives a stability condition that applies only to the distance-merging branch.
\item \textbf{Minimum-size and noise handling.} After merging, any resulting cluster (or any aggregation group left unmerged) that is smaller than a configured minimum size is not accepted as a valid cluster. Depending on configuration, its member points are either folded into a larger neighbouring cluster (a \emph{tiny group merge}) or, if not, labelled \emph{noise}: customers who are not assigned to any final cluster because no aggregation group containing them met the minimum-size or merging conditions required to become one. A noise label is itself a specific, recorded outcome of the aggregation and merging process, not a missing value or a data-quality problem, and — as used throughout Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology} and \ref{ch:results} — a noise assignment counts as a difference in outcome whenever two partitions are compared.
\item \textbf{Final cluster assignments.} Every customer's final label is either one of the merged cluster identifiers or the noise label.
\item \textbf{Provenance.} Because every one of the steps above is recorded — which aggregation group a customer's point fell into, that group's starting point, and the sequence of merge decisions that connected the group to its final cluster (or determined that it remained noise) — a customer's final label can always be traced back through this explicit chain. This traceable, step-by-step record is what this dissertation calls CLASSIX's \emph{provenance}: it is a property of how the partition was \emph{computed}, not a separately fitted description of the partition after the fact (contrast with ExKMC's post hoc trees, \S\ref{sec:resampling-explanation} and \S\ref{sec:explanation-results}).
\end{enumerate}

This staged process explains why CLASSIX is also sensitive to representation, through a different mechanism from K Means: adding nine standardised dimensions changes the covariance matrix used to compute the principal direction $u$ (stage 2), which changes every customer's projected score $s_i$ (stage 3), which changes the sorted order (stage 4) and therefore which points are examined together during aggregation (stage 5) — all before the full Euclidean aggregation radius is ever applied. A different principal direction is one concrete, mechanistic reason two representations of the same customers can produce different CLASSIX aggregation groups and, ultimately, different final clusters.

\subsection{Clustering candidates and selection}
\label{sec:candidates}

K Means candidates range over $k=2$ to $12$, using the seeding, initialisation and objective described above. CLASSIX candidates use the aggregation and merging process described above with Euclidean distance and sorting along the first principal direction; the aggregation radius $r$ ranges from 0.25 to 1.75 in increments of 0.05, the minimum cluster size takes values 1, 5 and 10, and group merging uses either the distance or the density rule defined above. The merge scale is 1.5, tiny groups can be merged into a neighbouring cluster, and post allocation (reassigning noise points after the main fit) is disabled. Each representation has 11 K Means candidates and 186 CLASSIX candidates. A recorded boundary diagnostic triggered a symmetric radius extension from 1.80 to 2.25 for both representations; the extension tests grid sensitivity and does not replace the primary selection.

Each method and representation reports an unconstrained silhouette optimum and a deployment-acceptable result. \emph{Silhouette} compares, for each point, its average distance to other points in its own cluster against its average distance to points in the nearest other cluster; it is higher when clusters are internally compact and well separated from each other, and its value lies in $[-1,1]$ \citep{rousseeuw1987silhouettes}. \emph{Davies--Bouldin} averages, over all clusters, the ratio of within-cluster scatter to between-cluster centroid separation, so \emph{lower} values indicate more compact, more separated clusters \citep{davies1979separation}. \emph{Calinski--Harabasz} is the ratio of between-cluster to within-cluster variance (scaled by degrees of freedom), so \emph{higher} values indicate more separation relative to internal spread \citep{calinski1974dendrite}. A candidate is internally valid only when at least two nonnoise clusters remain and all three indices are finite. Deployment acceptability further requires 2 to 12 nonnoise clusters, no more than 20 per cent noise and no single nonnoise cluster larger than 95 per cent of nonnoise observations. Selection maximises silhouette, then prefers lower Davies--Bouldin, higher Calinski--Harabasz, a smaller largest cluster share, less noise, fewer clusters and finally the lexical parameter record. Runtime is measured but never selects a model. Keeping the unconstrained result reveals when an attractive internal score is produced by a partition that is unsuitable under the stated operational constraints — for example, a partition that places nearly every customer in one cluster and a handful of outliers in a second (\S\ref{sec:paired-results}).

Internal indices exclude observations labelled as noise, so CLASSIX index comparisons refer to each partition's nonnoise subset. Cross-representation partition comparisons retain every purchaser because a noise assignment is itself a difference in assignment. Fixed $K$ comparisons use the same value from 2 to 12. Fixed CLASSIX comparisons use the same radius, minimum size and merge rule. Independently selected deployment partitions are compared using three partition-agreement statistics. The \emph{adjusted Rand index} (ARI) counts, over all pairs of customers, whether the two partitions agree on whether the pair is in the same cluster or different clusters, then corrects this count so that a random relabelling of either partition scores approximately 0 while identical partitions score 1 \citep{hubert1985partitions}. \emph{Normalised mutual information} (NMI) measures how much information one partition's labels carry about the other's, expressed on a 0-to-1 information-theoretic scale and normalised here by the arithmetic mean of each partition's own entropy, so the statistic does not automatically reward a larger number of clusters. \emph{Variation of information} (VI) is a complementary information-theoretic \emph{distance} between two partitions, reported in nats (natural-log units, because entropy is computed with the natural logarithm here): a value of 0 means the partitions are identical, and larger values mean less shared information \citep{meila2007vi}. Because cluster labels are arbitrary integers assigned independently within each fit, a \emph{Hungarian match} is used before building transition tables: this is an assignment algorithm that finds the one-to-one correspondence between two partitions' labels that maximises total overlap, so that "cluster 2 in the compact fit" can be meaningfully compared with "cluster 2 in the rich fit" \citep{kuhn1955hungarian}. The resulting migration share describes changed assignments under that matched correspondence and does not imply that numeric labels identify persistent customer types across representations.

Business \emph{addressability} is evaluated for selected CLASSIX and K Means clusters through a documented audit rather than subjective cluster names. A nonnoise cluster must contain at least 50 customers and one per cent of nonnoise customers, retain at least 80 per cent category coverage, and differ from the global median by at least half an interquartile range on two to four named features. Passing this audit shows only that a cluster can be expressed as a limited set of query conditions with adequate coverage; it does not demonstrate intervention effectiveness, revenue change or human comprehension (\S\ref{sec:explanation-results}, \S\ref{sec:addressability-hierarchy}).

\subsection{Resampling and explanation evaluation}
\label{sec:resampling-explanation}

Fixed-parameter overlap-resampling \emph{stability} uses ten pairs of independent 80 per cent samples without replacement. The same seed pairs are used for both representations and methods. Each sample refits its transformations and clustering with the full-data deployment parameters. Adjusted Rand index is calculated only on purchaser identifiers present in both members of a pair. The statistic measures sensitivity to sampled customers and, for K Means, initialisation, \emph{conditional on the selected configuration} — that is, it measures whether the same fixed recipe reproduces a similar partition when applied to a slightly different 80 per cent of customers. It does not measure stability of model \emph{selection} (whether the same configuration would be chosen again from the full grid), calendar-time stability, or stability on an external cohort.

The CLASSIX explanation retains public aggregation group assignments, starting point indices, final labels and cluster sizes, in the sense defined in \S\ref{sec:classix-math} above. Merge membership is reconstructed from public group and final assignments because version 1.5.1 does not expose a documented complete edge list. Every exported provenance label is checked against the selected main result for the same visitor. The trace is an explanation of the fitted CLASSIX partition and does not claim that a group boundary is a human-validated customer rule.

ExKMC approximates K Means labels under five deterministic 80 per cent training and 20 per cent test splits, as a post hoc decision tree fitted \emph{after} the K Means partition already exists (\S\ref{sec:lit-explainable}). The primary full-cohort selection gives four K Means clusters in both representations, so $K$ is fixed at four before splitting. Log transformations, standardisation, the K Means reference and the ExKMC tree are fitted using training rows only. The test target is the label returned by the fitted K Means predictor. This estimates explanation fidelity conditional on the primary $K$ choice rather than nested selection of $K$. The \emph{leaf budget} — the maximum number of terminal nodes (leaves) the tree is allowed to grow to, with each leaf corresponding to one exported rule — ranges from $K$ to four times $K$. The main comparison fixes the budget at $K$, while the wider range measures the fidelity and complexity tradeoff. A supplementary analysis selects $K$ from 2 to 12 using each training split alone. Fidelity is the proportion of exact label agreements between the tree and the held-out K Means labels. Complexity is reported through effective leaves, depth, path conditions and features. Rule thresholds are exported in both standardised and original units.

\subsection{Projection filtering and complexity}
\label{sec:projection-filtering}

Every candidate CLASSIX fit must decide, for a potentially large number of pairs of observations, whether one lies within the aggregation radius of another (stage 5 of \S\ref{sec:classix-math} above). Checking every pair by computing the full $d$-dimensional Euclidean distance would be correct but wasteful, because CLASSIX has already sorted every observation by its one-dimensional projected score before this step is reached. This section states and proves \emph{why} that one-dimensional sorted order can be used to rule out some full-distance comparisons in advance, without ever discarding a comparison that the exact Euclidean check would have accepted: sorting observations by their projection allows some Euclidean distance checks to be ruled out before the full distance is ever calculated, because a large enough gap between two projected scores already guarantees that the full Euclidean distance must exceed the aggregation radius. This matters for the dissertation's argument because it clarifies exactly what computational mechanism produces CLASSIX's aggregation groups and, through them, its provenance — a mechanism structurally different from ExKMC's post hoc tree fitting, and one whose computational cost is worth stating precisely rather than assumed to be linear.

Let processed (standardised) observations be $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ ($d=3$ or $12$, as above), let $u\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be the unit sorting direction from \S\ref{sec:classix-math} (so $\lVert u\rVert_2=1$), and let $s_i=u^{\mathsf T}x_i$ be observation $i$'s projected score. CLASSIX orders the scores and begins a group at a starting point $c$ (itself one of the $x_i$, with score $s(c)=u^{\mathsf T}c$). Its aggregation radius, in the standardised coordinates used throughout this dissertation, is $r$.

\begin{proposition}
If a later point (in sorted order) satisfies $s_i-s(c)>r$, then it cannot satisfy $\lVert x_i-c\rVert_2\leq r$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, applied to the unit vector $u$ and the difference vector $x_i-c$,
\[
\lvert s_i-s(c)\rvert
=\lvert u^{\mathsf T}(x_i-c)\rvert
\leq \lVert u\rVert_2\lVert x_i-c\rVert_2
=\lVert x_i-c\rVert_2,
\]
using $\lVert u\rVert_2=1$. So the (one-dimensional) score gap is never larger than the (full $d$-dimensional) Euclidean distance. If the strict inequality $s_i-s(c)>r$ holds, the displayed bound forces $\lVert x_i-c\rVert_2>r$ as well, so $x_i$ cannot lie within the aggregation radius of $c$. The implementation includes equality in both the score window and the exact squared-distance comparison, so this filter never rejects a point the exact Euclidean check would have accepted (a mathematical false negative), apart from ordinary floating-point rounding.
\end{proof}

In plain terms: if two observations are already separated by more than $r$ along the one unit projection direction $u$, their full Euclidean distance cannot possibly be $\leq r$ either, because projecting onto $u$ can only ever shrink a distance, never enlarge it. The later observation can therefore be safely ruled out of the current aggregation group using only its one-dimensional score, without ever computing the full $d$-dimensional distance. Sorting observations by their projected score groups points that are likely to be close together in the sorted order, so this rule lets CLASSIX skip the full-distance calculation for the (often many) pairs that are already far apart along the principal direction, while still checking the full Euclidean distance for every pair the rule cannot rule out.

This filter gives \emph{exact} pruning for a fixed sorted order — it never changes which points end up in the same group compared with checking every full distance directly — but it does not make the resulting greedy group assignment independent of that order: two different valid sort orders of the same data could, in principle, walk through points in a different sequence and produce different starting points and groups. If $n$ observations produce $q$ aggregation groups, aggregation retains at most $n(n-1)/2$ exact point comparisons, and distance merging retains at most $q(q-1)/2$ starting-point comparisons. The worst-case distance arithmetic is therefore
\[
O\bigl((n^2+q^2)d\bigr)
\]
in addition to principal-direction estimation and scalar sorting. It is important to distinguish three different things that might be meant by "how much work CLASSIX does": this $O((n^2+q^2)d)$ bound is a \emph{worst-case theoretical bound} on the number of exact distance comparisons that could be retained after filtering; the CLASSIX package's own internal counter, separately, records the number of aggregation comparisons \emph{actually} retained on the specific observed data (\S\ref{sec:explanation-results} reports 131 and 559 aggregation groups for the compact and rich fits); and the \emph{empirical runtime} recorded in \S\ref{sec:benchmark-results}'s benchmark is a wall-clock measurement on one machine. None of these three quantities substitutes for either of the others, and in particular the package counter — an empirical quantity observed on this dataset — is not a proof that CLASSIX's aggregation step runs in linear time in general; it only reports what happened on the data actually fitted.

Conditional determinism requires more than a fixed random seed. A positive leading eigengap (defined precisely in \S\ref{sec:stability-certificate} below) fixes the one-dimensional principal subspace, while the implementation's sign convention fixes its orientation once that convention's defining quantity is separated from zero. The sorted order is fixed when relevant scores are distinct. Exact ties require an explicit stable secondary order or an order-invariance check. Radius, merging, minimum-size and nearest-eligible-group decisions must also avoid unresolved equalities. These conditions distinguish deterministic execution on the observed input from a global statement about all nearby inputs — a distinction taken up directly, for small perturbations of the input, in the next section.

\subsection{A local stability certificate}
\label{sec:stability-certificate}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results}'s sensitivity analysis (\S\ref{sec:sensitivity-perturbation}) perturbs the feature representation with small amounts of Gaussian noise and checks whether the fitted CLASSIX partition changes. Because CLASSIX begins by sorting observations along the principal direction $u$ (\S\ref{sec:classix-math}, stages 2--4), one plausible source of instability under such a perturbation is that the perturbation itself rotates the principal direction, which would reorder the sorted scores and could, in turn, change which points are aggregated together. This section derives \emph{sufficient conditions} under which that does not happen: conditions under which every finite decision that defines a distance-merging CLASSIX fit — the sorted order, the aggregation-window membership tests, and the merge-distance tests — remains exactly unchanged after a sufficiently small perturbation, so that the fitted partition itself is provably unchanged. This complements, but is distinct from, the empirical perturbation results reported in \S\ref{sec:sensitivity-perturbation}: the certificate below is a mathematical guarantee under stated conditions, whereas \S\ref{sec:sensitivity-perturbation} reports what was actually observed when the perturbation was applied.

Let $S$ be the processed (standardised) data's covariance matrix, with leading eigenvalues $\lambda_1\geq\lambda_2$. Define the \emph{eigengap} $\gamma=\lambda_1-\lambda_2>0$: intuitively, a larger eigengap means the leading eigenvector (the principal direction $u$) is more sharply preferred over the second eigenvector, so a given amount of perturbation to the covariance matrix produces a smaller rotation of $u$ — this intuition is made precise by the Davis--Kahan theorem below. Let a perturbation change the covariance matrix by $\Delta S$, and let $\widehat u$ denote the resulting perturbed principal direction, sign-aligned with the original $u$ (so that $\widehat u\to u$, rather than $\widehat u\to -u$, as the perturbation shrinks to zero). \emph{The Davis--Kahan theorem} bounds how far a matrix's leading eigenvector can rotate under a bounded perturbation of the matrix, as a function of the perturbation's size and the eigengap; it does not say anything about any of CLASSIX's subsequent aggregation, merging or noise decisions, only about this one geometric quantity, the direction $u$. When $2\lVert\Delta S\rVert_2<\gamma$ (the perturbation is small relative to the eigengap), the theorem gives the conservative bound \citep{davis1970rotation}
\[
\sin\angle(\widehat u,u)
\leq \frac{2\lVert\Delta S\rVert_2}{\gamma}.
\]
In words: the sine of the angle between the original and perturbed principal directions is bounded by twice the perturbation's size, divided by the eigengap — so a larger eigengap directly buys a tighter bound on how much the sorting direction can rotate for a given perturbation. After sign alignment, this angular bound converts to a bound on the vectors' Euclidean distance, $\lVert\widehat u-u\rVert_2\leq\sqrt{2}\sin\angle(\widehat u,u)$. If every processed point changes by at most $\varepsilon_x$ under the perturbation and every processed point has Euclidean norm at most $B$, then the resulting change in any point's projected score is bounded by
\[
b_s=\varepsilon_x+\sqrt{2}B\,\frac{2\lVert\Delta S\rVert_2}{\gamma}.
\]
This score-change bound $b_s$ combines two sources of movement: $\varepsilon_x$, the point's own direct displacement under the perturbation, and the second term, the extra score movement caused purely by the sorting direction itself rotating.

The remaining symbols state, for each of CLASSIX's finite decisions in turn, how large a safety margin that decision currently has against the perturbation. Let $g_s$ be the minimum gap, in the sorted order, between the projected scores of adjacent observations (the sorted-order safety margin); let $g_o$ be the absolute value of the score that the implementation's orientation (sign) convention is evaluated against (the sign-flip safety margin). Let $g_w$ be the minimum margin, over every relevant pair, between a projected score difference and the aggregation-window boundary $r$ (the aggregation-acceptance safety margin). Let $g_r$ be the minimum margin between any tested full-distance aggregation check and $r$ (the exact-distance safety margin). For the distance-merging branch specifically, let $g_m$ be the corresponding minimum margin between any tested starting-point distance and the active merge threshold (the merge-decision safety margin); let $b_r$ and $b_m$ bound how much those two distance tests can themselves change under the perturbation, analogously to $b_s$ above.

\begin{proposition}
The fitted partition under distance merging is unchanged by the perturbation if $g_s>2b_s$, $g_o>b_s$, $g_w>2b_s$, $g_r>b_r$ and $g_m>b_m$, provided that the representatives, eligible groups, minimum-size decisions, tiny-group rule, post-allocation rule and all relevant tie decisions remain fixed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first inequality preserves every relevant pairwise score order because each endpoint of a compared pair can move by at most $b_s$, so a gap larger than $2b_s$ cannot be closed or reversed. The second preserves the package's orientation sign, because the sign-defining quantity cannot cross zero if it starts more than $b_s$ away from it. The third fixes window membership (whether a point falls inside or outside the aggregation radius, judged by score) because each relevant score difference can move by at most $2b_s$ (the sum of two endpoints' movements). The fourth preserves every accepted or rejected full-distance aggregation comparison, since that comparison's value cannot move past $r$ if it started more than $b_r$ away from it; the fifth preserves every merge comparison by the same argument applied to $b_m$ and the merge threshold. With the branch (distance merging), the set of representatives, and the eligible comparison set all held fixed by these five conditions, every one of CLASSIX's underlying finite decisions is unchanged, and unchanged finite decisions reproduce exactly the same aggregation groups, the same merge components, and therefore the same final labels.
\end{proof}

This certificate must not be read as more than it is. It is \emph{local}: it applies only within a neighbourhood of the original fit small enough that the stated margins exceed the stated bounds, not globally across arbitrary perturbations. It is \emph{sufficient, not necessary}: a fit could still happen to be unchanged even when one of the five margin conditions fails, since the conditions are deliberately conservative. It is \emph{conditional} on the branch and on every other finite decision (representatives, eligible groups, minimum size, tiny-group and post-allocation rules, tie-breaking) remaining fixed as stated. It applies \emph{only to the distance-merging branch}: it does not cover density merging, whose merge decision depends on the local density of intervening points rather than on a single starting-point distance, and would require separately derived margins that this dissertation does not attempt. The Davis--Kahan bound itself controls only the rotation of the principal direction $u$; it says nothing on its own about aggregation, merging, minimum-size or noise decisions unless the additional finite margins above are also shown to hold. Most importantly for this dissertation's argument, this proposition is \textbf{not a general proof that CLASSIX is stable}, and it is \textbf{not a stability certificate for the rich-representation CLASSIX result reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}}: that selected configuration uses \emph{density} merging (\S\ref{sec:paired-results}, Table~\ref{tab:customer_results}), which this certificate explicitly does not cover. The empirical Gaussian-perturbation results reported in \S\ref{sec:sensitivity-perturbation}, and the empirical customer-resampling results reported in \S\ref{sec:paired-results}, are therefore complementary to, but distinct from, this mathematical statement — one is a proof under stated sufficient conditions for one branch of the algorithm, the other is an observation of what actually happened when the fitted rich-representation (density-merging) partition was perturbed. Neither substitutes for the other.

\subsection{Benchmark and robustness designs}
\label{sec:benchmark-robustness}

Nine labelled datasets provide an algorithm-level benchmark for CLASSIX, K Means, and two further methods used only in this benchmark: DBSCAN, a density-based clustering algorithm that groups points found in dense, connected regions of the feature space and labels sparse points as noise, and Ward's method, an agglomerative hierarchical clustering algorithm that repeatedly merges the pair of clusters that would cause the smallest increase in within-cluster variance \citep{ester1996dbscan,ward1963hierarchical}. Candidate selection uses only internal indices and deployment guards, exactly as described in \S\ref{sec:candidates}; the datasets' reference labels are reserved for the subsequent ARI and NMI evaluation and never influence selection. K Means results average five fixed seeds before parameter selection. Ward, DBSCAN and CLASSIX store one structural row per candidate, while repeated fits audit label identity and runtime on the recorded environment. This empirical identity check is not a global determinism theorem.

The rich representation undergoes twelve leave-one-feature-out fits (one fit with each of the twelve named features removed in turn). PCA — principal component analysis, which re-expresses the standardised features as a smaller set of uncorrelated linear combinations ordered by the variance each explains, without using any cluster labels \citep{pearson1901pca,hotelling1933pca} — retains at least 90 per cent of standardised variance and is compared with the named feature space on structure and stability only, never on rule quality, because its rotated components no longer correspond to the twelve named, business-meaningful variables. \emph{CLASSIX perturbation} adds independent Gaussian noise, at a stated standard deviation, to every standardised feature value, refits CLASSIX with the selected parameters on the resulting perturbed data, and measures agreement (by ARI) between the perturbed and unperturbed fits; this dissertation uses standard deviations 0, 0.000001, 0.0001, 0.001 and 0.01, with ten independent noise seeds at every nonzero level. Session counts are rebuilt with inactivity gaps of 15, 30 and 60 minutes, in place of the primary 30-minute definition (\S\ref{sec:feature-engineering}). A negative control appends independently permuted rich-only dimensions to unchanged compact features: this demonstrates that added dimensionality and standardisation alone \emph{can} alter geometry even when the added columns carry no customer-aligned information, but it is a single deterministic existence demonstration rather than a variance decomposition, and it is not used to estimate what share of the compact-to-rich effect is attributable to dimensionality as such (\S\ref{sec:sensitivity-dimensionality}). These diagnostics retain the primary selected configurations unless a section explicitly reports complete feature reselection.
