\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This dissertation asked five research questions (\S\ref{sec:intro-rq}) about how changing the information used to represent the same 11,719 purchasers affects observable and explainable cluster structure. On RQ1 and RQ2, compact and rich representations produce substantially different assignments for both K Means and CLASSIX: the two representations' independently selected partitions agree with each other at an adjusted Rand index close to what unrelated random partitions of the same customers would score, whether or not the number of clusters happens to match, and this holds even though no external ground truth exists to say either partition is "correct" (\S\ref{sec:intro-content}). The compact space gives higher silhouette values, while the rich space gives a more balanced K Means partition and many more CLASSIX aggregation groups. Matched-parameter CLASSIX settings also give low or variable agreement. Fixed-parameter overlap-resampling remains high within each representation, so conditional repeatability — a configuration reliably reproducing its own partition — does not imply agreement across representations or stability of model selection; these are answers to different questions (\S\ref{sec:paired-results}).

On RQ3 and RQ4, the explanation results reveal a representation-dependent frontier. At four leaves, compact ExKMC fidelity is 0.9448 and rich fidelity is 0.9650 with equal tree complexity. At sixteen leaves, compact fidelity rises to 0.9924 while rich fidelity reaches 0.9747. CLASSIX provenance remains exact, yet the rich fit requires 559 aggregation groups compared with 131 — a difference in computational granularity, not necessarily in human interpretability (\S\ref{sec:explanation-results}). Of eight selected clusters across both methods and representations, only one rich K Means cluster passes the addressability audit; the remaining seven fail on size, category coverage, or distinctiveness despite several having passed every weaker check (internal validity, resampling stability) that precedes addressability in the hierarchy set out in \S\ref{sec:addressability-hierarchy}. Richer behaviour increases descriptive reach without guaranteeing simpler explanations, superior internal scores or deployment-ready groups.

On RQ5, the sensitivity programme (\S\ref{sec:sensitivity}) rules out several specific alternative explanations of these findings — numerical fragility, the exact session-window choice, and dimensionality as such — while identifying category behaviour as the single largest, specifically attributable driver of the rich-representation effect.

Taken together, this evidence supports one bounded claim: representation is not a \emph{neutral input} to clustering (\S\ref{sec:lit-representation}) for this cohort, this feature contract, and these two algorithms. It determines the geometry against which clustering quality, stability and explainability are all judged, and changing it changes conclusions that might otherwise be attributed to the algorithm or the customers alone. This claim does not extend further than that: the benchmark (\S\ref{sec:benchmark-results}) establishes only that the implementations and metric path behave sensibly across varied labelled structures, not that any one algorithm is generally superior; the local stability certificate (\S\ref{sec:stability-certificate}) states only the margins sufficient for the distance-merging branch, not a general stability guarantee for CLASSIX; and no result in this dissertation measures whether representation-dependent clusters would respond differently to an actual business intervention. Future work should test whether such clusters predict prospective responses under complete category and value data, and whether analysts understand and act on the exported provenance and rules. Until then, technical queryability is not realised business value.
